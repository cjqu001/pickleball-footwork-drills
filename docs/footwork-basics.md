# Footwork Basics

This page explains the movement terms used in the drill cards. No tennis background is needed.

Provenance: original instructional summary. It draws on general concepts described in [S1](resources.md#s1-improving-your-pickleball-footwork) [S2](resources.md#s2-pickleball-coaches-manual), and [S5](resources.md#s5-a-closer-look-at-pickleball-footwork-with-michael-loyd). Wording and cues are original.

## Court words

- Kitchen - the non-volley zone. It is the area 7 feet deep on each side of the net. The edge of it is the kitchen line.
- Transition area - the space between the kitchen line and the baseline. Players move through it on the way to the net.
- Baseline - the back line of the court.
- Feeder - the partner who tosses or hits balls to the player doing the drill.

## Ready position and balanced posture

- Feet a little wider than your hips.
- Knees slightly bent. Bend at the knees, not the waist, so your head stays up.
- Weight on the balls of your feet, not your heels.
- Paddle up in front of your body.
- Chest and eyes facing the ball.
- Balanced means you could move in any direction without first shifting your weight back to center.

## Split step

- A split step is a small unweighting or light hop.
- You time it around the moment your opponent hits the ball.
- You land balanced, so you can move as soon as the direction is clear.
- Keep it small. A big jump wastes time.
- The exact timing changes with the shot. You may barely leave the ground during a fast exchange at the net. You may have a more visible hop when the ball comes from the baseline.
- There is no single correct timing for every shot. Aim to be balanced and ready as the ball leaves the opponent's paddle.

## Lateral shuffle

- Use a shuffle to move a short distance side to side.
- Step with the foot nearest the ball, then bring the other foot along.
- Keep your feet from crossing or clicking together.
- Keep your chest mostly facing the net.
- This is the usual choice for small moves along the kitchen line.

## Crossover and running steps

- Use a crossover step when the ball is too far for one or two shuffles.
- Turn your hips toward the ball and cross the far leg in front.
- For an even longer move, run with normal running steps.
- Shuffle and crossover are both correct. Pick by distance, time, and the shot you need next.
- A cue like "shuffle at the kitchen" is a good default for short balls. It is not a ban on crossover steps.

## Adjustment steps

- Adjustment steps are small, quick steps just before contact.
- They set your distance from the ball.
- Too close is crowded. Too far is stretched. Both make contact harder to control.
- Aim to contact the ball in front of your body at a comfortable arm distance.

## Moving forward through the transition area

- Move forward when you have time, for example after your own soft shot lands.
- As the opponent gets ready to hit, slow down and split step.
- Hit from a balanced stop. Do not hit while still running if you can avoid it.
- Continue forward only when the next ball gives you time.
- This stop-and-go pattern often takes more than one step forward.

## Recovery

- Recovery means moving to a good spot for the next ball after you hit.
- The right spot depends on where the ball is and where your partner is.
- In doubles, partners shift together toward the side where the ball is. This keeps the middle covered.
- After a wide ball, a diagonal path back toward the middle usually covers more angles than moving straight back.
- There is no single fixed center point you always return to.
- Recover under control. Be balanced again before the opponent hits.

## Turn and run for a lob

- A lob goes over your head and lands behind you.
- Turn your hips and shoulders toward the side the ball is going.
- Run forward along a path beside the ball, not straight back under it.
- Track the ball over your shoulder.
- Avoid backpedaling for more than a step or two. Running backward blind is a common cause of falls.
- Call to your partner. One player takes the ball and the other covers the open space.

## Rehearsal versus reaction

- Rehearsal means practicing a movement you already know is coming. Solo drills and predictable feeds are rehearsal.
- Reaction means moving after you read a real ball. Random partner feeds train reaction.
- Learn a pattern with rehearsal first. Then test it with random feeds.
- Good rehearsal does not prove good reaction. Both are needed.
